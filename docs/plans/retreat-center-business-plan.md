# Business Plan: A Hawaiʻi Retreat Center Supporting HHP

*Working name: [NEEDS: name]. Built from my capstone proposal, "From Study to Readiness." Every market and cost-of-inaction figure below comes from that deck and the sources it cites. Every operating figure is an assumption, marked as one, until I replace it with a quote.*

## 1. Executive Summary

Hawaiʻi has about 104,765 veterans. Applying the national PTSD rate, roughly 7,650 of them have PTSD right now. Today, a veteran who wants breakthrough-therapy care has to leave the islands to get it. The Heroic Hearts Project (HHP) already sends veterans to psychedelic-assisted retreats outside the United States [verify how HHP describes its program]. The weak link for a Hawaiʻi veteran is everything around the retreat: preparation before they fly out, and integration after they come home, which is where outcomes are either kept or lost.

The center fills that gap in two phases.

- **Phase 1 (2027–2029): legal today, asset-light.** We run preparation and integration retreats for HHP veterans and their families in rented Hawaiʻi retreat venues, with nothing controlled on site. We also host the veteran-advocate and testimony training from my capstone. Ten veterans per cohort: 6 cohorts in Year 1, 10 in Year 2, 16 in Year 3. That is 320 veterans over three years.
- **Phase 2 (after FDA approval and state authorization): treatment site.** The same program, staff and relationships become a site that delivers FDA-approved MDMA- or psilocybin-assisted therapy in Hawaiʻi, paid for by VA Community Care, TRICARE and insurers. My capstone's Ask A, a JABSOM-led readiness task force, is the policy path that makes Phase 2 possible.

**Funding need:** about $261,000 in Year 1, $513,000 in Year 2 and $784,000 in Year 3, each including a 10% reserve. The fully loaded cost is $4,000–$4,700 per veteran. The organization is a nonprofit and veterans attend at no cost, so the money comes from grants, donors, sponsors and an HHP partnership, not from fees.

**Why it is worth funding:** the capstone deck estimates PTSD's excess cost among Hawaiʻi's veterans at about $196 million a year, or $25,684 per veteran with PTSD, in 2018 dollars (Davis et al. 2022). One fully loaded seat costs less than a fifth of one year of that excess. That comparison is the reason the problem is worth funding. It is not a promise of savings: integration support is not the treatment, and Phase 1 will not claim the outcomes the treatment trials report.

## 2. The Problem and the Opportunity

**Need.** The figures come from the capstone deck.

- 6,398 U.S. veterans died by suicide in 2023, about 17.5 a day. Hawaiʻi lost 20 in 2022 (VA 2025 report; Hawaiʻi data sheet 2022).
- Veterans with PTSD are 2.4× likelier to be unemployed and 3.8× likelier to be disabled (Pietrzak et al. 2023).
- PTSD care has seen 20 years of little innovation, and SSRIs and talk therapy often fail.
- Two compounds, MDMA and psilocybin, hold FDA Breakthrough Therapy designation. In the MAPP2 trial, 71% of MDMA-assisted therapy participants no longer had PTSD, against 48% on placebo plus therapy (Nature Medicine 2023).

**Demand is already proven, and the money is leaving.** Veterans pay $4,000–$8,000 for a five-day stay in Tijuana, before airfare from Hawaiʻi (KPBS 2024). Oregon's licensed psilocybin program served 5,935 clients in 2025, which scales to about 2,000 a year at Hawaiʻi's population (Oregon Health Authority 2025; order-of-magnitude only). Hawaiʻi patients spent more than $230M on out-of-state cancer care in 2021–23 (UH Cancer Center 2026), which shows the same pattern: when care isn't local, the dollars go too.

**The gap the center fills.** A veteran from Hilo or Waiʻanae who goes to an HHP partner retreat today prepares alone and comes home to no local peer group. That is a gap in the care itself, and a local organization can close it legally now.

**Market size (Phase 1).** About 7,650 Hawaiʻi veterans have PTSD; that floor excludes active duty, first responders and families. Phase 1 serves 60–160 veterans a year: under 1% of that number in Year 1 and about 2% by Year 3. Capacity is limited by funding, not demand. ## 4. Programs and Services (Phase 1)

| Program | Format | Who | What it delivers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preparation series | 4 virtual sessions in Year 1; an in-person 2–3 day retreat from Year 2 if funded | Veterans accepted by HHP, before departure | Intentions, expectations, medical and safety briefing, peer cohort formed before travel |
| Integration retreat | 5 days / 4 nights, cohort of 10 | Veterans 2–8 weeks after their HHP retreat | Peer-led integration circles, a licensed clinical lead, ocean and ʻāina-based activity, sleep, nutrition, a plan for the next 6 months |
| Follow-up circles | 6 monthly virtual sessions | Every graduate | Keeps the cohort together; early warning if someone is struggling |
| Family program | 1–2 days per integration cohort [Year 2] | Spouses and partners | What changed, what to expect, how to support |
| Advocate training | 1-day sessions during session season | Graduates who choose to advocate | The 3-minute story, testimony writing and delivery: the capstone's Deliverable 2 |

**Scope boundaries, written down so they hold:**
- No controlled substances on site, and no referral to illegal use in Hawaiʻi. Phase 1 serves veterans going through HHP's legal-abroad pathway only.
- Clinical work is done only by Hawaiʻi-licensed clinicians (psychologist, LCSW or LMHC) acting within their license. Integration coaching is described as peer support, not therapy.
- Every cohort has a written crisis and suicide-risk protocol: screening at intake, a clinician on call, the Veterans Crisis Line (988, press 1), and a warm handoff to VA care.
- Stories are shared at each person's comfort level, nothing is filmed without written consent, and reports are anonymized. These rules carry over from the HB training (deck slide 18).

**Culture and place.** Native Hawaiian practitioners are partners, paid and credited, and ʻāina-based activities happen only with their guidance and permission. The deck names Native Hawaiian and cultural leaders as part of the coalition for culturally informed care. This is about partnership, not program imagery, which keeps it consistent with the messaging rules. [NEEDS: named partners.]

## 5. Marketing and Outreach

Veterans attend free, so "marketing" means two things: getting referrals and raising money.

- **Referrals:** HHP intake first. Then the Hawaiʻi Office of Veterans' Services, VFW and American Legion posts, Vet Centers, unit and alumni networks, and word of mouth from graduates, which is the channel that matters most.
- **Funders:** veteran-focused foundations, Hawaiʻi family and community foundations, and corporate sponsors with a military presence in Hawaiʻi. Individual donors are reached through one annual event and graduates' stories, shared with consent.
- **Message:** the deck's "Say/Avoid" list applies. Lead with outcomes and the cost of inaction; never debate.

## 6. Operations

- **Facilities:** rent existing licensed retreat venues per cohort, rotating islands so neighbor-island veterans aren't always the ones who fly. No property in Phase 1: leasing waits for steady funding and Phase 2 licensing requirements. [NEEDS: three venue quotes, per person per night, all-inclusive.]
- **Staffing:**
  - Year 1: a part-time program coordinator (0.5 FTE), contracted clinicians and peer facilitators per cohort, and me as unpaid founder and director alongside my day job.
  - Year 2: a half-time paid executive director.
  - Year 3: a full-time executive director.
  - Facilitators are HHP alumni trained in peer integration.
- **Board:** 5–7 seats: a veteran, a clinician, a Native Hawaiian community leader, a finance and nonprofit-governance member, an HHP liaison, and a JABSOM or academic member once Phase 2 planning begins.
- **Measurement:**
  - Validated measures at intake, discharge, 3 months and 6 months: PCL-5 for PTSD symptoms and PHQ-9 for depression [confirm with clinical lead].
  - Follow-up attendance, graduates who become advocates, and testimonies submitted.
  - Results reported to funders in aggregate only.

## 9. Phase 2: Treatment Site

**Gates.** Phase 2 does not start until all three of these are true:
1. The FDA has approved MDMA- or psilocybin-assisted therapy, and the drug is rescheduled. [NEEDS: current FDA and DEA status at the time of writing.]
2. Hawaiʻi has the readiness framework (task force recommendations, provider rules) that my capstone's Ask A is working toward.
3. Payers, meaning VA Community Care, TRICARE and HMSA or other insurers, will reimburse the course.

**What changes:** a leased dedicated site, DEA registration, whatever certification the FDA's approval requires of treatment sites, and a full clinical staff. The Phase 1 cohort model, integration program and referral network carry straight over. That is the reason to build them first.

**Size of the prize.** The capstone deck's uptake scenarios put care spending at $2.3M, $4.6M and $9.2M a year for 380, 765 and 1,530 veterans, priced at the $6,000 Tijuana midpoint. A U.S. FDA-approved course will likely cost more, so the dollar figures are a floor and the veteran counts are the real constraint. These are scenarios, not forecasts. Phase 2 gets its own financial model once the gates are visible.

**Research bridge.** Before approval, the site can partner with JABSOM to host clinical trials, which the deck identifies as a federal funding opportunity. This is the earliest legal way to deliver the therapy itself in Hawaiʻi.

## 10. Risks and Mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Seen as facilitating illegal drug use | Nothing controlled on site; HHP's legal-abroad pathway only; written scope; legal review before launch |
| A participant in crisis | Intake screening, clinician on call, written suicide-risk protocol, 988 press 1, VA handoff |
| HHP partnership doesn't happen or changes | Get the MOU before fundraising; integration also serves veterans from clinical trials and other legal pathways |
| Fundraising falls short | Cohort-by-cohort scaling, two-cohort cash rule, low fixed cost in Year 1 |
| Clinical scope creep (coaching that becomes therapy) | Licensed clinical lead, written role boundaries, insurance matched to scope |
| Cultural appropriation or backlash | Paid Native Hawaiian partners on the board and in programming; no ceremony imagery |
| Founder capacity (I have a day job) | Coordinator from Day 1, paid director in Year 2, board with real responsibilities |
| Phase 2 never arrives | Phase 1 stands on its own: it supports veterans whatever happens to the law |
