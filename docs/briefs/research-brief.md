# Research Brief: The Economic Cost of Unpriced Groundwater Extraction in Central Maui

## The Challenge

The Kahului and Pā'ia aquifer sectors in Central Maui, Hawai'i, are being pumped far beyond their sustainable yield. As of the most recent state data (12-month moving average through May 2026), the Kahului Aquifer is being drawn at roughly 776% of its official sustainable yield, and the Pā'ia Aquifer at roughly 217% — both figures higher than they were six months earlier, indicating a worsening trend rather than a temporary spike (CWRM, reported via Maui Now, Sept. 2026). Mahi Pono, the region's largest agricultural operator, profits from the crops this water produces but pays only the cost of pumping, not for the water itself. The immediate cost is visible in the state's own regulatory record: Central and South Maui remain under a Stage 1 Water Shortage declaration (CWRM Water Resource Bulletin, Sept. 2025), and the Commission on Water Resource Management (CWRM) has acknowledged it currently lacks the statutory authority to regulate pumping in this basin at all, because it has never been designated a Ground Water Management Area. The longer-term cost falls on future water users, including a newly approved 170-home development already planning to draw from the same over-drafted Pā'ia aquifer, and on the broader municipal water system — Maui County's Department of Water Supply draws from the same aquifer complex to serve Wailuku, Kahului, Kīhei, Wailea, and Makena, and was already operating at 98–100% of capacity as of August 2026. The environment itself is at risk: sustained over-pumping thins the freshwater lens and allows saltwater intrusion, a documented trend in Central Maui aquifers since the 1980s (USGS, 2022), and over-drafted aquifers can also physically compact, permanently reducing their future storage capacity.

## Economic Concepts

The problem is a combination of common-pool resource dynamics, negative externality, and mispricing.

**Common-pool resource:** a landowner or lessee may pump, but the Kahului and Pā'ia aquifer sectors are shared by every user drawing from them — Mahi Pono's agricultural operation, Maui County's municipal system, and now private wells proposed for new residential development. No single user's restraint protects the resource unless every user restrains.

**Negative externality:** rising groundwater extraction degrades water quality for everyone drawing from the same source. Aggressive pumping thins the freshwater lens and allows saltwater intrusion. The cost falls on Maui's municipal water system and its customers, on future development already planning to depend on the same water, and on stakeholders such as the Hawaiian Homes Commission's East Maui representative, who has formally raised concern that withdrawals are exceeding sustainable yield limits.

**Mispricing:** groundwater carries no price attached to extraction in an undesignated basin, so Mahi Pono's pumping decisions never register a scarcity signal.

**Why the market won't self-correct:** Water itself carries no cost in the Kahului and Pā'ia aquifer sectors — its value is seen only in the crops it produces. Because no extraction limit currently applies to either aquifer, there is little incentive for any single user to conserve; restraint by one pumper does not protect the aquifer for anyone else, so the incentive runs toward extraction rather than conservation. Hawai'i's water law does not grant landowners a private right to groundwater at all: the state holds all water in trust for the public under the Public Trust Doctrine established in *Waiāhole* (94 Hawai'i 97, 9 P.3d 409 (2000)), and courts have held there are "no vested rights in the use of public water." On paper, this is a stronger protective framework than a private-rights system. In practice, the public trust's protections only take effect once CWRM designates a basin as a Ground Water Management Area and begins issuing water use permits. Until that designation occurs, the doctrine imposes no enforceable limit on extraction — CWRM itself has acknowledged it currently lacks the statutory authority to regulate pumping here for exactly this reason, despite Kahului being pumped at a level nearly nine times above the 90%-of-sustainable-yield threshold the State Water Code identifies as cause for designation (HRS §174C-41).

## Planned Analysis

1. Establish the groundwater baseline from USGS and CWRM's 2019 Water Resource Protection Plan.
2. Document the pumping-vs-sustainable-yield trend using CWRM's 12-month moving average data, to show the magnitude and trajectory of depletion.
3. Build a standard negative-externality diagram (marginal private cost vs. marginal social cost of extraction).
4. Evaluate the return-flow-recharge dispute directly: Mahi Pono argues the 2019 sustainable-yield figures are outdated because they don't account for irrigation water seeping back into the aquifer. This is a real methodological question the analysis needs to address, since it bears directly on whether the overdraft claim holds.
5. Compare policy alternatives — designation vs. status quo, and metering/caps/fees in isolation vs. combined — before settling on a recommendation.
6. Verify every number against CWRM's or USGS's original publication before it goes in the final paper.

## Decision Question

Should CWRM designate the Kahului and Pā'ia aquifer sectors as a Ground Water Management Area under HRS §174C-41, and if so, what water use permit framework — metering, extraction caps, and scarcity-based pumping fees — should apply to large-volume users?

## Working Thesis

*(Preliminary — subject to revision after evidence is examined.)*

CWRM should designate the Kahului and Pā'ia aquifer sectors as a Ground Water Management Area, given both aquifers are pumped far beyond the statutory 90%-of-sustainable-yield trigger. Once designated, CWRM should implement mandatory metering, a basin-wide extraction cap set from sustainable-yield targets, and a progressive scarcity fee applied to large producers — defined as any entity pumping more than 100,000 gallons per day (0.1 mgd), a threshold set above comparable domestic-use exemptions in Washington (5,000 gpd) and Oregon (15,000 gpd) and equal to roughly 10% of Kahului's sustainable yield. Existing agricultural operations would not be grandfathered, but would receive notice to comply within one year. Residential and small-business users would remain permanently exempt.

## What Would Change My Mind

1. Evidence that Mahi Pono's return-flow-recharge argument is correct — that irrigation seepage genuinely offsets pumping enough that the official sustainable-yield figures are wrong. This would undercut the core overdraft claim, not just the policy prescription.
2. Evidence that metering, caps, and fees do not measurably reduce groundwater usage where they've been tried elsewhere.
3. Evidence that the depletion rate is primarily driven by something other than agricultural pumping — for example, municipal use or drought-driven recharge decline independent of extraction volume.
