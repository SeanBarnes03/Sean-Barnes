# Research Brief: Groundwater Extraction and the Contracting Recharge Subsidy in Central Maui

## The Challenge

The Kahului and Pā'ia aquifer systems, two of the four systems in Central Maui's Central aquifer sector, are being pumped above what the State calls their sustainable yield — but how far above is itself contested, and the contest is where the paper starts. The figures CWRM reports through its own primary channel: Kahului withdrew 5.2 Mgal/d in 2023 against a sustainable yield of 1 Mgal/d, and Pā'ia withdrew 6.8 Mgal/d against a yield of 7 — meaning Pā'ia was, on that reporting, slightly *under* its limit as recently as 2023 (USGS, "Analysis of Groundwater Flow in the Central Aquifer Sector," Jan. 2025, citing CWRM written communication, 2024; attached to CWRM Staff Submittal B2, May 19, 2026). Press coverage of a 12-month moving average through May 2026 reports far higher numbers — roughly 776% for Kahului and 217% for Pā'ia, or 7.760 and 15.175 Mgal/d (CWRM, reported via Maui Now, Sept. 2026). Those two accounts cannot both be routine: the 2026 figures for these two systems alone exceed the 16.7 Mgal/d reported for the entire four-system sector in 2023. Reconciling them is the first analytical task, not a detail. Those percentages need a caveat the same agency supplies. CWRM's 2019 Water Resource Protection Plan notes, for both aquifers, that the sustainable yield "ignores significant importation of surface water (return irrigation)" from outside the aquifer system, and that this "explains the ability to withdraw freshwater from the aquifer at significantly higher rates than the sustainable yield without apparent negative impacts (i.e. rising chloride concentrations or decreasing water levels)" (WRPP 2019, Table F-11). The overdraft figures therefore measure extraction against a benchmark the State says was never built to carry it. That is the paper's starting problem, not its conclusion. Two facts frame the scale. Reported withdrawals from the Central aquifer sector peaked at 120 Mgal/d in 1996 under sugarcane and fell to between 8 and 17 Mgal/d across 2017–23 after cultivation ceased in 2016 — today's pumping is a fraction of what this sector once carried. But the same 2016 cessation "altered the water budget of the area by eliminating substantial areas of cultivated sugarcane that previously contributed to groundwater recharge" (USGS, 2025). The aquifer is being asked to supply less than it once did, from a recharge base that shrank when the plantations closed. Mahi Pono, the region's largest agricultural operator, profits from the crops this water produces but pays only the cost of pumping, not for the water itself. The immediate cost is visible in the state's own regulatory record: Central and South Maui remain under a Stage 1 Water Shortage declaration (CWRM Water Resource Bulletin, Sept. 2025), and the Commission on Water Resource Management (CWRM) has acknowledged it currently lacks the statutory authority to regulate pumping in this basin at all, because it has never been designated a Ground Water Management Area. The longer-term cost falls on future water users, including a newly approved 170-home development already planning to draw from the same over-drafted Pā'ia aquifer, and on the broader municipal water system — Maui County's Department of Water Supply draws from the same aquifer complex to serve Wailuku, Kahului, Kīhei, Wailea, and Makena, and was already operating at 98–100% of capacity as of August 2026. The environment itself is at risk: sustained over-pumping thins the freshwater lens and allows saltwater intrusion, a documented trend in Central Maui aquifers since the 1980s (USGS, 2022), and over-drafted aquifers can also physically compact, permanently reducing their future storage capacity. Table F-11's "no apparent negative impacts" sits in tension with that intrusion record, and resolving the tension is part of the analysis rather than something to assume past. What makes the question urgent now is that the imported surface water doing the recharging is itself contracting: East Maui drought has cut deliveries, which removes the return flow that made withdrawals above sustainable yield survivable and pushes the same operator further onto the pumps.

One more finding complicates every per-aquifer percentage above. The boundaries separating Kahului, Pā'ia, Makawao and Kama'ole "were not drawn on the basis of known hydrogeologic boundaries" but from topographic, political and surface-ditch features, and simulated groundwater flow crosses the line between Kahului and Pā'ia, so "withdrawal from one aquifer system will affect groundwater flow in other aquifer systems" (USGS, 2025). A 776% figure for Kahului is a ratio against a boundary the State's own hydrologists say may not describe the water. CWRM approved a three-year, $442,000 joint study with USGS on May 19, 2026 to resolve exactly this, and USGS confirms that "no wells are currently available in the study area to characterize the thicknesses of freshwater or the transition zone." The basin is being argued over with instruments nobody has yet installed.

## Economic Concepts

The problem is a combination of common-pool resource dynamics, negative externality, and mispricing.

**Common-pool resource:** a landowner or lessee may pump, but the Kahului and Pā'ia aquifer systems are shared by every user drawing from them — Mahi Pono's agricultural operation, Maui County's municipal system, and now private wells proposed for new residential development. No single user's restraint protects the resource unless every user restrains.

**Negative externality:** rising groundwater extraction degrades water quality for everyone drawing from the same source. Aggressive pumping thins the freshwater lens and allows saltwater intrusion. The cost falls on Maui's municipal water system and its customers, on future development already planning to depend on the same water, and on stakeholders such as the Hawaiian Homes Commission's East Maui representative, who has formally raised concern that withdrawals are exceeding sustainable yield limits. The honest qualifier: CWRM has not yet observed that degradation in these two aquifers, because return irrigation has been masking it. The externality is real in mechanism but not yet demonstrated in the chloride record, and the paper has to say so.

**The recharge subsidy:** the return irrigation that keeps these aquifers viable is imported East Maui surface water, and nobody is charged for the service it performs. It is an unpriced input propping up an unpriced resource — which means the buffer shrinks whenever drought or a delivery decision cuts the imports, and no user faces a signal when it does. A subsidy nobody prices is a subsidy nobody notices disappearing.

**Mispricing:** groundwater carries no price attached to extraction in an undesignated basin, so Mahi Pono's pumping decisions never register a scarcity signal.

**Why the market won't self-correct:** Water itself carries no cost in the Kahului and Pā'ia aquifer systems — its value is seen only in the crops it produces. Because no extraction limit currently applies to either aquifer, there is little incentive for any single user to conserve; restraint by one pumper does not protect the aquifer for anyone else, so the incentive runs toward extraction rather than conservation. Hawai'i's water law does not grant landowners a private right to groundwater at all: the state holds all water in trust for the public under the Public Trust Doctrine established in *Waiāhole* (94 Hawai'i 97, 9 P.3d 409 (2000)), and courts have held there are "no vested rights in the use of public water." On paper, this is a stronger protective framework than a private-rights system. In practice, the public trust's protections only take effect once CWRM designates a basin as a Ground Water Management Area and begins issuing water use permits. Until that designation occurs, the doctrine imposes no enforceable limit on extraction — CWRM itself has acknowledged it currently lacks the statutory authority to regulate pumping here for exactly this reason, despite Kahului being pumped, on every reporting vintage available, well above the 90%-of-sustainable-yield threshold the State Water Code identifies as cause for designation (HRS §174C-41).

## Planned Analysis

1. Establish the groundwater baseline from USGS and CWRM's 2019 Water Resource Protection Plan, reading Table F-11's aquifer notes as part of the baseline rather than a footnote to it.
2. Reconcile the two reporting vintages before charting either: USGS/CWRM's 2023 withdrawals (Kahului 5.2, Pā'ia 6.8, sector 16.7 Mgal/d) against the 12-MAV figures reported for May 2026 (7.760 and 15.175 Mgal/d). Either Pā'ia's draw more than doubled in under three years — which would itself be the story — or the two series are not measuring the same thing.
3. Document the pumping-vs-sustainable-yield gap. This is the paper's primary figure: reported withdrawals against the sustainable-yield lines, on a long enough baseline to show the 1996 peak of 120 Mgal/d and the post-2016 collapse, annotated with the Table F-11 caveat so the figure carries the dispute instead of hiding it.
4. Settle what the sustainable-yield numbers actually measure. The return-flow argument is not only Mahi Pono's — CWRM published it in 2019, and its May 19, 2026 Action Plan recommends updating recharge estimates and sustainable yields "with new and best information." If the denominator is wrong, every percentage in the public debate is wrong, including mine.
5. Test the intrusion claim rather than assert it: chart chloride concentration at Kahului and Pā'ia production wells over time. CWRM has no deep monitor well in either aquifer — the Pā'ia and Kama'ole wells are pre-construction — so transition-zone depth cannot be charted directly. If chloride is flat, that is evidence against my own externality claim and it goes in the paper.
6. Trace the recharge subsidy: East Maui surface-water deliveries over time against Central Maui groundwater pumping, to test whether the pumps are replacing imports rather than expanding production.
7. Build a standard negative-externality diagram (marginal private cost vs. marginal social cost of extraction) to carry the theory.
8. Compare policy alternatives — designation now, designation of the whole Central sector, waiting for the USGS study to report, and status quo — and metering/caps/fees in isolation vs. combined, before settling on a recommendation.
9. Verify every number against CWRM's or USGS's original publication before it goes in the final paper, and label advocacy testimony as testimony.

## A Note on the Data

The May 2026 12-month moving average figures (7.760 Mgal/d for Kahului, 15.175 for Pā'ia)
come from press reporting of CWRM data, not from a CWRM publication I have been able to
obtain. The originals are public by records request, but the request cannot be answered
inside this project's timeline. I am using the reported figures and saying so: every
appearance of them in the paper is attributed to the reporting rather than to CWRM
directly, and the analysis does not rest on them alone — the 2023 withdrawals published
by USGS from CWRM's own written communication are the primary-sourced anchor, and the
gap between the two vintages is treated as a finding rather than smoothed over.

## Decision Question

Should CWRM designate Central Maui groundwater as a Ground Water Management Area under HRS §174C-41 — and at what unit, the Kahului and Pā'ia aquifer systems or the whole Central aquifer sector whose internal boundaries its own hydrologists question — or should it wait for the USGS flow study it funded in May 2026? If it designates, what water use permit framework — metering, extraction caps, and scarcity-based pumping fees — should apply to large-volume users?

## Working Thesis

*(Preliminary — subject to revision after evidence is examined.)*

The case for designation does not rest on the 776% and 217% figures. CWRM's own plan says those denominators exclude the return irrigation that has been holding these aquifers up; its hydrologists say the boundaries the ratios are drawn against may not describe the water; and its own reported 2023 withdrawals are far lower, with Pā'ia under its yield. It rests on the fact that nobody — including CWRM — can currently say how much of that buffer is left, while the imported surface water producing it contracts under drought and the demands on it grow. Designation is worth doing first as the measuring instrument: water use permits compel the reporting that produces the series nobody now has, and CWRM has said the sustainable yields themselves need updating. The $442,000 USGS study answers a different question — where the water flows — on a three-year horizon, and produces no obligation on any pumper when it reports. Designation and study are complements, and the argument for acting first is that the study will describe a basin whose withdrawals stayed unregulated while it ran. Once designated, CWRM should implement mandatory metering, a basin-wide extraction cap set from sustainable-yield targets, and a progressive scarcity fee applied to large producers — defined as any entity pumping more than 100,000 gallons per day (0.1 mgd), a threshold set above comparable domestic-use exemptions in Washington (5,000 gpd) and Oregon (15,000 gpd) and equal to roughly 10% of Kahului's stated sustainable yield. Existing agricultural operations would not be grandfathered, but would receive notice to comply within one year. Residential and small-business users would remain permanently exempt.

## What Would Change My Mind

1. Evidence that return-flow recharge fully offsets current withdrawals — that at present delivery volumes the aquifers are in balance, with chloride and water levels showing no adverse trend. CWRM's own Table F-11 already concedes part of this, which is why the thesis no longer leans on the overdraft percentages. What would finish the job is evidence that the offset holds at the *reduced* delivery volumes now prevailing under drought. That would remove the urgency and leave designation as a data-collection argument only.
2. Evidence that metering, caps, and fees do not measurably reduce groundwater usage where they've been tried elsewhere.
2a. Evidence that the May 2026 12-MAV figures are a reporting artifact rather than a real increase — that Pā'ia did not in fact go from 6.8 Mgal/d in 2023 to 15.175 in 2026. The direction of my argument would survive, but the urgency behind acting before the USGS study reports would not.
3. Evidence that Mahi Pono's groundwater pumping rises roughly one-for-one with shortfalls in East Maui surface-water deliveries — that pumping climbs only as deliveries fall. That would make extraction a drought-driven substitution rather than a response to water being free, and it would change the instrument rather than the finding: a scarcity fee would tax a substitution the pumper cannot avoid, and the remedy would move toward surface-water reliability and allocation instead of price.
